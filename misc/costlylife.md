# What Makes Life Ever-increasingly Costly

In current times, many of us struggle to afford a home and the basic expenses of daily life.
Many of us are frustrated at how things are, and wonder why the world is turning into an unaffordable train of debt.

We wonder why wealth is so unevenly distributed, and why those with the biggest capital are those who desire most of what is left.
Why is it that businessmen and banking families seek power and economic growth endlessly, and landlords hoard more houses than ever, while we struggle to merely own one ourselves?

There is a psychological explanation behind all of this, why those with the most resources are the greediest for more, and why this pattern is as old as humanity itself.
My text aims to explain what causes an unfair, uneven and costly life from a perspective of the human mind and why we evolved like this.

## Clarifying The Problem

When I mention uneven wealth, inflation, housing costs and similar problems I am in a general sense talking about our struggle to find the resources we need as well as the difficulty in affording it.

This might seem like a modern problem that has become worse than ever, and it indeed has become significantly worse than the last decades, but truth is that our society suffers from these struggles for hundreds if not thousands of years.
Our struggle repeats endlessly in a cycle that lasts for decades: sooner or later, we can no longer comfortably afford the essentials to our daily life, while those who can afford entire mansions take more from us than ever.

## Endless Growth

People with capital and wealth tend to seek more capital and wealth, without ever settling for what they would consider their comfortable point.
Instead of stopping at a reasonable level, they seek infinite growth.

For investors and business people, at some point the goal is no longer to obtain wealth to live comfortably, but to play the game.
The game is the whole challenge and adventure of investing and trading, with all its risks and rewards, variables and strategies.
Increasing wealth is a challenge that attracts people who seek this kind of excitement and pleasure.

This feeling is very hardwired in us, even if we would believe it does not apply to us.
A good example of that is gambling, an activity that more people than ever are hooked into.
In gambling, we play a highly risky game or uncertainty followed by total loss or incredible gains.
Even if we gain big with our bets we tend to not want to settle, instead we want to keep betting to grow even more.

Growth maintains the challenge and fuels the drive for risk, excitement, adrenaline, strategy, complex economical systems and more.
We love the game and we not only want to continue playing it but we want to increase the level.
In investment and business, this often comes at the expense of others no longer gaining a share for sustaining a comfortable life.

We instinctively hoard and seek endless growth as a survival strategy that we gained throughout countless generations that predate humanity itself.
When an essential resource is obtained in much higher quantity, we want to hoard all of it to make sure we have plenty of backup in case of having to go through a period where we can no longer obtain any more.
We observe this when we eat addictive foods, especially sugar: we eat a bit then we want even more and the cycle does not stop, even if we got plenty already for the day. 

## The Power Game

For some, it is not about the game of business as much as it is about the game of power.
This is more commonly seen in banking families and political leaders than businessmen or investors.
The line between the money game and the power game is blurry because the different drives do not differ much in their method, but they differ more in the final outcome.
Instead of having money as the product of your efforts, you have dominance.

Being at the top of the social hierarchy is a necessity some have because it originates from a survival strategy.
We perceive social status as superiority, and superiority implies higher chances of survival.
In competitive environments, we survive by winning the race rather than by cooperating and working together.

Many of us have this necessity but at a smaller cost.
We worry about how others perceive us, and we want to be seen as of higher status.
Some of us present themselves as the masters of a field to be at the top, other people show up on expensive clothes, and so on.
For many of us, if we were at the level of a politician we would act similarly to how they do, even if we criticise them now.

## Physical Distance

For many people with capital and power, metaphorically they look down on us from a tower above the clouds.
They make decisions that affect us but they are nowhere next to us, and that partly contributes to the damage they cause.

This physical distance causes an emotional distance.
We lose compassion for those far from us ("out of sight, out of mind").
Many of us suffer from this as well: look at how hostile and conflictuous we are on the internet, especially social media and chat platforms.
We no longer see people, friends and family when interacting with other people, we now see profile pictures, labels, identities and ideologies.

We lose empathy and compassion and now see people as enemies, as the opposition, or at best we don't care about them and how we could affect them.

## Extrinsic desire

The necessities and desires I mentioned above, wealth, power, status, are all extrinsic desires.
Extrinsic drive is a will that motivates us based on the final outcome.
It drives us to do something not because we like doing it or we like what it produces but because it brings us something else in return, usually money or status.

Intrinsic desire defines motivation that comes from the love of doing the task itself.
Intrinsic desire is common in technical domains, hobbies, arts and creative activities.
These tasks and projects come from a desire or vision deep inside us, we either love the activity itself or the creative or technical result it creates (or both).

For intrinsically-driven people, desire for wealth tends to only go as far as to fulfill basic necessities as well as their resources and tools for their hobby or work.
Even if people like this participate in investment, they eventually come to a comfortable point where they stop, and spend their efforts entirely in their passion.

Extrinsically-driven people will not stop, instead they will seek more growth and more capital.

## Satisfying Large Consumption

People who live minimalist lives require a smaller income to sustain themsleves.
Many people, powerful or not, live expensive lives originating from excessive consumption and materialism.

To these people, large incomes are not seen as an unnecessary extra but the basic minimum, because in a way it is.
To them, it is the minimum because they need large incomes to sustain their lifestyle.

While this might result in a millionaire investing in 30 houses so he can sustain his mansion and private jet, this also happens at a smaller scope.
Many people cut corners and seek positions of higher income to instead sustain expensive materials that are not nearly as expensive as a mansion or an airplane but are still needless luxuries.

## A Sustainable Life

It is possible to live a life that does not contribute to these problems.
The bad news is that it almost never happens to the people that need it the most, instead the people that live sustainable lives are already destined to do so, because it comes from how their mind works and what drives them.

Intrinsic desire drives us for proactive and creative pleasures that do not interfere with the economy in a serious way, rather than seeking the growth and status that harms others.
Proximity to everyday life also keeps us grounded, and minimalism keeps our expenses at check.
Discipline and awareness about the risks of addiction and growth allow us to invest better and efficiently not in stocks or housing but in life.
It applies to the smallest things, even our physical health and safety against gambling and other predatory strategies.

## Conclusion

Our life is expensive because those with the most resources have a notably larger thirst for more resources.
This comes from a combination of psychologic effects as well as how our minds evolved for survival.
A big part of the problem comes from our instinct to hoard as much as we can, or the drive for a game of business and investment with high risk and high reward, or to be at the top of the social hierarchy for survival and safety.

This problem is deep-rooted in the human mind, and so it has existed for much longer than we could remember, and will continue to exist.
Positions of power and wealth attract those with the personality traits most vulnerable to these psychological traps, and we take the burden.
Many of us would act the same, we just only lack the capital to do so.

The best each of us can do is to acknowledge these patterns and understand how to avoid them and live a more grounded life driven by intrinsic desires and moderation.
It's important to understand why things are the way they are, but we shouldn't overthink it too much beyond the necessary to sustain ourselves, it will make us resentful.

We might not be able to change the world alone, but we can vote with our position and choices, we make our stand.
